The Sun bends starlight into a telescope 550 AU long.

Light from a distant planet that grazes the Sun is deflected by 1.75 arcseconds and meets the optical axis far beyond Pluto. Out there a spacecraft sees the planet magnified about a hundred billion times. Move the sliders, or drag the spacecraft along the axis.

From bent rays to a focal line

A ray passing at distance b from the lens centre crosses the axis at z = b²/2Rs. Rays that touch the limb focus first, at zmin. Every point beyond is in focus: that is the focal line. Rays closer in hit the star.

Drag the spacecraft along the axis to set its distance.

An oblate Sun turns the focus into an astroid

The Sun is slightly flattened by its rotation (quadrupole moment J2). Instead of a single focal point, the receiver plane holds a four-cusped caustic. Inside it the telescope sees four images of the planet on the Einstein ring; outside it, two.

Wave optics: the focus is centimetres wide

Treated as a wave, the focal line has a Bessel profile, J₀²(kθEρ). The bright core is only a few centimetres across, so even a 1 m telescope collects many rings at once. The core widens with wavelength and with distance.

Which planets should a solar-lens telescope image first?

Each known nearby planet gets a feasibility score out of 100, computed for a spacecraft at 650 AU. The score weighs how much light reaches the detector (30%), how long the image takes to scan (25%), how close the target is to the ecliptic, where it is cheaper to fly (25%), and how little the planet's rotation blurs the scan (20%).
